# Project page copy

## When to use this

Use this when you write or revise the public page for a project: the page a founder sends to a friend, posts in a group chat, or links from a profile. It also fits any single page with one goal, such as a waitlist page or a page for one offer.

Do not use it for a long article, an email or a social post. Those have their own skills.

## Before you start

Read these before writing a word:

1. The idea in the founder's own words. Their phrasing is the voice. If they wrote "stuff" and "fix", the page does not say "solutions" and "resolve".
2. The positioning notes, if the project has them: who it is for, the problem, what is different.
3. Anything real you can point to: what exists today, who is building it, the stage, a first customer's words. Note the record each fact comes from.
4. The one step the page asks for. A page has exactly one: follow the project, join the waitlist, book a call, or buy.

If a fact you need is missing, do not fill the hole with a guess. Write the page with a marked gap, like `[NEED: one sentence from a real customer]`, and list the gaps in your hand-back.

Then decide which of three situations you are in, and say so in one line:

| Situation | What you were given | Your job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Blank | A one-line idea and little else | Build the argument from the idea and the records. You choose the angle. You never choose the facts. |
| B. A voice is present | A draft or notes with an opinion or a way of talking | Keep their argument and their words. Fix the buried point, the vague lines and the order. |
| C. Point edit | "Fix the headline" | Change what was asked and the least around it. Return the rest untouched. |

## Steps

1. **Write the page in pieces, not in one pass.** The pieces are: headline, subhead, the button, then each section's heading and body. For each piece, write two or three versions, keep the best, fix its weakest word, and move on.
2. **Headline: say what it is and who it is for.** A stranger should be able to repeat it. Patterns that work:
   - "{What they get} without {the thing they dread}"
   - "The {kind of thing} for {who}"
   - A question that names the problem in the reader's words
3. **Subhead: one or two sentences that add the specifics** the headline left out: how it works, or what happens first.
4. **The button says what the reader gets.** Verb, then the thing: "Join the waitlist", "Get the first lesson", "Book a 15-minute call". Never "Submit" or "Learn more".
5. **Sections, in this order, each making one point:**

   | Section | Its job | For a week-one project |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | The problem | Show you understand their situation | Two or three sentences in the reader's own words |
   | What it is | Connect the thing to the outcome | Three points at most, each a result, not a feature |
   | How it works | Make it feel simple | Three steps |
   | Who is building it | Earn trust without proof you do not have | The founder's name and why they care, one true sentence |
   | Questions | Answer the doubts that stop people | Price, timing, "is this real yet" |
   | The step again | Repeat the one action | Same button, same words |

6. **Run every line through three questions.**
   1. Can the reader picture it? A specific scene beats an abstraction.
   2. Could it be proven true or false? "Lesson notes in your inbox an hour after class" can. "Best-in-class" cannot.
   3. Could a competitor print the same sentence? If yes, rewrite it from a fact only this project has.

   A line that passes two gets sharpened. A line that passes one or none gets rewritten or marked `[NEED: ...]`.
7. **Be plain most of the time.** A page gets one or two lines with punch, usually the headline. If every line tries to land, none does.
8. **Remove the tells of machine-written copy.** No "It's not X, it's Y". No lists of things that are absent ("no setup, no fees, no hassle"). No question you answer yourself ("The result? Faster."). No "In today's world", "Whether you're", "Say goodbye to", "Unlock". No dashes in headlines. At most one group of three per section.
9. **State only what the records support.** When a claim the founder would like is not supported, do not write it and do not drop it silently. Name it, say what is missing, offer the nearest true line, and say what evidence would allow the original.
10. **Read the whole page once from the top** as someone who has never heard of the project. Cut anything that does not move them toward the one step.

## What good looks like

A founder wrote: "i want to help people in my town find someone to fix their bike without going to the big shop."

Headline: "Get your bike fixed by someone down the street."
Subhead: "Tell us what is wrong. A local repairer replies with a price, usually the same day. Starting in Bend, Oregon."
Button: "Join the waitlist"
The problem: "The shop is booked for two weeks. Your chain has been skipping since March."
Who is building it: "Built by Dana, who has fixed bikes in a garage on Elm Street for six years."
Question: "Is it running yet? Not yet. The first repairs start when 50 people in Bend have joined."

Every line is something a person can picture, and every claim is one the founder told us.

## Output

Hand back:

```
Situation: A, B or C, with one line of evidence
Page copy:
  Headline:
  Subhead:
  Button:
  Section: <heading>
    <body>
  ...
Two other headlines, each with one line on why
Page title (under 60 characters) and description (under 155 characters)
Gaps: each [NEED: ...] with what would fill it
Sources: the record behind each factual claim
```

For a point edit, return only the changed lines with one line of reason each.

## Check before you hand it over

- The page asks for one step, and the button says what the reader gets.
- A stranger could repeat the headline after one read.
- Every factual claim has a record behind it, and nothing is invented: no numbers, customers, quotes or deadlines that are not real.
- No line would work unchanged on a competitor's page.
- None of the tells in step 8 remain. Search for them.
- No sentence over 20 words that could be two.
- It reads in the founder's vocabulary, not a brochure's.
- Anything that is not real yet is said plainly ("starting soon"), never implied to exist.

## What needs the founder

The founder approves the page before it is public, and approves any change to what the project promises: a price, a date, a guarantee. Prepare the page with the gaps listed, so approving it is one read.

## If a tool is missing

If you cannot publish the page yourself, hand back the finished copy in the output format above and say plainly that it has not been published. Do not describe it as live.
