# Terms, privacy and what must be published

## When to use this

Use this the first time a project's page collects an email address, the first time it takes a payment or a booking, and whenever a connected tool changes what is collected about visitors. It tells you which documents and notices the project's situation calls for and what goes in each.

This is information, not legal advice, and the hand-back says so. You prepare the texts. The founder reads them and publishes them.

## Before you start

Read from the records. What a project like this usually does is not evidence of what this one does.

1. What the page really collects: each form field, and where each value goes.
2. The real list of connected tools: email sending, payments, analytics, embedded video, chat. Each may receive personal data.
3. What is sold, how it is delivered, and whether any charge repeats.
4. Where the founder is, and where visitors and buyers are likely to be. If the records do not say, assume some are in the US, the EU and the UK.
5. Who the seller is (the founder as a person, or a registered business) and the contact details they are willing to publish.
6. Whether the project is aimed at children. If it is, stop and recommend a lawyer.

The rules below are summaries as of 2026. Before relying on one, check the current rule at an official source and record the source and the date.

## Steps

1. **Find the project's situation.** More than one row can apply.

   | Situation | What has to be published or done |
   | --- | --- |
   | The page collects nothing | Who is behind the page and how to reach them. Claims that are true |
   | It collects an email address (waitlist, followers, newsletter) | A privacy notice linked beside the form. A plain line at the form saying what the person will receive. A record of when and how each address was given |
   | It sends marketing email | A working unsubscribe in every message. The sender's real name. A postal address in every message to US recipients |
   | It takes payment or bookings | Terms shown before the buyer pays: what is sold, the full price with any tax and delivery charge, when it is delivered, how to cancel, the refund rule, how to reach the seller |
   | It takes preorders | The delivery date, and a refund offer if the date is missed. In the US, ship by the promised date, or within 30 days when none was given, or offer a refund |
   | It sells online to consumers in the EU or UK | A 14-day right to change their mind on most purchases, stated before purchase, with the exceptions that apply (for example services already carried out, digital content already delivered with the buyer's agreement, made-to-order goods, bookings for a set date) |
   | It charges on a repeating basis | The renewal terms stated before purchase, the buyer's clear agreement to them, and a way to cancel that is as easy as signing up |
   | It sets cookies or trackers the page does not need in order to work | For visitors in the EU or UK, consent before they are set, with a real choice to refuse. The UK exempts some low-risk uses such as basic visit counts. Setting none is the simplest route |
   | It has visitors or customers in the EU or UK | The privacy notice also covers why each kind of data is used, how long it is kept, which tools receive it and in which countries, and the person's rights to see, correct, delete and object |
   | It has visitors in California | A posted privacy notice, required of any commercial site that collects personal information from California residents, whatever its size |

2. **Write the privacy notice from what is real.** One short section per question, in plain words: who we are and how to reach us, what we collect and when, why, which tools receive it, how long we keep it, your choices and rights, how this notice changes. Describe each connected tool by what it does. If the notice says something the page does not do, or leaves out something it does, change the notice or the page before publishing.
3. **Write short terms.** For a first sale one page is enough: who the seller is, what is sold, price and payment, delivery or scheduling, cancellation and refunds, what happens if something goes wrong, which law applies, how to get in touch. Promise only what the founder can keep.
4. **Set the email basics.** The form says what the person is signing up for. Marketing goes only to people who asked for it. Every message carries an unsubscribe that works in one step, and a request is acted on the same day even where the law allows longer. Marketing email to US recipients shows a postal address in the footer: a street address, a post office box or a registered private mailbox. A home address is valid, and every recipient will see it, so put the choice to the founder with the cost of a box as an Estimate.
5. **Settle cookies by removing the need.** List every cookie and tracker the page sets. The ones the page needs in order to work call for no consent. For each of the others, either take it off the page or prepare a consent banner that sets nothing until the visitor agrees and makes "no" as easy as "yes". At a first sale, a visitor count that sets no cookie is usually enough.
6. **Keep the documents in step with the product.** When a tool is connected or removed, or the offer changes, update the notice and the terms through the product's change-proposal flow, and tell the founder in one line what changed.
7. **Say when a lawyer is worth it.** Recommend one when the project is aimed at children, handles health or financial details, sells food, cosmetics, alcohol or anything else that is regulated, or passes personal data to others for advertising.
8. **Hand the founder a checklist.** One line per item: what it is, where it goes on the page, and what the founder has to do (read, fill a blank, publish). Mark each blank as `[NEED: ...]`.

## What good looks like

Maya's sewing classes have a waitlist form (name and email), bookings paid by card through a payment tool, one email update a month and no analytics cookies. Maya is in Oregon, and a few followers are in the UK.

```
This is information, not legal advice.
1. Privacy notice (drafted). Goes under the waitlist form and in the page footer. Covers name, email and booking details; the email tool and the payment tool; kept until you ask us to delete; rights for UK visitors. You: read and publish.
2. Line at the form (drafted): "Join the list to hear about new class dates, about once a month. Unsubscribe any time." You: approve.
3. Class terms (drafted, one page). Price, what is included, a full refund up to 48 hours before, what happens if a class is canceled. Shown before payment. You: read, change the refund rule if you wish, publish.
4. Email footer. Unsubscribe link is in place. Postal address: [NEED: an address you are willing to show; a post office box is about $5 to $30 a month, Estimate]. You: choose.
5. Cookies. The page sets only what it needs, so there is no banner. Nothing to do.
Sources: official guidance on US commercial email and UK privacy rules, checked Nov 15, 2026.
```

## Check before you hand it over

- The hand-back opens with the line saying this is information, not legal advice.
- Every statement in the privacy notice matches something the page or a connected tool really does, and nothing collected is left out.
- Every tool that receives personal data is in the notice.
- The terms promise only what the founder can deliver.
- Marketing email has an unsubscribe, the sender's real name and, for US recipients, a postal address.
- For EU and UK visitors, only cookies the page needs are set before consent.
- Each rule cited has a source and a date checked.
- You published nothing, accepted nothing and filed nothing.

## What needs the founder

Publishing every document. Choosing the refund rule, the postal address and which law applies. Agreeing to any tool's own terms or data agreement. Registering with a data regulator where one is required. Paying for a lawyer or a mailbox.

## If a tool is missing

If you cannot publish, hand over the drafts with the place each one goes, marked "not published". If you cannot read the list of connected tools, ask your lead for it and mark the privacy notice "incomplete: tools not confirmed". If you cannot check a current rule, mark it "not verified" and say what to check.
