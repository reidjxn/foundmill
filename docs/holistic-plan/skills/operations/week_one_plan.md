# A project's first week

## When to use this

Use this when you plan, order or review work for a project in its first week, and again whenever a young project has a long task list and nothing to show outside the product.

It sets the order of the week. Writing to the founder, choosing their daily task and the path to a first payment each have their own skill.

## Before you start

Read the project's state from its records, never from what you remember of an earlier round:

1. The idea in the founder's words, and the kind of project: a service, a physical product, or software or content.
2. What exists. Is the public page drafted? Is it live? Has anyone outside seen it?
3. What the project can really do today. Mark each capability yes or no: publish the page, send email from the project's address, publish to a social account, take a payment or a booking, keep code somewhere (a connected repository).
4. What already waits on the founder, and how old each item is.
5. The founder's starting money and weekly hours, if the records have them.

The week is built the way it is because of what happens on real projects. In measured use, a new project got about two dozen agents and as many as 17 notes on its first day. Founders answered one decision at most, and most did not come back. Two thirds of agent sessions ended blocked, mostly code work with nowhere to push it and campaigns waiting for an answer. A first week that works is short, and everything in it can finish.

## Steps

1. **Fix each day's outcome before you look at the task list.** The week is a sequence of real outcomes. A later one starts when the one before it is done or is in the founder's hands.

   | Day | Outcome | The team does | The founder's one thing | Done looks like |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | 1 | The page is live and shared once | Drafts the page with one step for a visitor: follow, or join the waitlist | Publishes the page, then sends the link to one person | A live address, and one share the founder reports |
   | 2 | Five people who might want it | Writes one line on who it is for, and a short personal message | Sends the link to five people or places | Five sends reported, every reply recorded |
   | 3 | A first message to followers | Drafts a short update for the people who followed the page | Approves the send, or sends it themselves | A real send in the records, or the draft marked not sent |
   | 4 | A first post | Drafts one post for the place those five people already spend time | Posts it from their own account, or approves it | A link to the live post |
   | 5 | A way to be paid or booked | Prepares the offer, two or three price options and the page section | Decides the price and connects the way to be paid | A link a stranger could use |
   | 6 to 7 | Replies and the week's review | Records replies, drafts follow-ups, reviews the week | Has one conversation with someone who replied | Notes from one real conversation |

   Days are steps in a sequence. If the founder is away for two days, day 2 is still next. If nobody has followed the page by day 3, day 3 is a second round of day 2 with five new people.
2. **Check every task against the capability list.** Start a task only if the tools the project has today can carry it to its end. A task that would stop at a missing tool is left out. Note what is missing in one line and plan the nearest task that can finish.
3. **Leave later work for later.** Open no department or line of work the stage does not need yet. In week one, do not propose or create tasks for company registration or other legal filings, paid ads, hiring plans, investor material, a brand system beyond what the page needs, analytics beyond a visitor count, or a second channel. If such a task already exists, put it last and give the reason in one line, such as "not needed before the first sale".
4. **Code waits for a place to live.** While no repository is connected, plan no code task. Until then the product is the page and conversations: a waitlist, a booking by reply, a service delivered by hand, a preorder. Say once, in one line, that building starts when the founder connects a place for code. Do not repeat it in later notes.
5. **Keep day one to one question at a time.** The specification and the roadmap are drafted on day one with the page. The founder is asked about the page first, and the sign-offs follow one by one. Before they are signed off, start groundwork only, as the Chief of Staff role already requires.
6. **Respect the start of distribution.** Until the founder has started distribution, the team drafts and the founder sends from their own accounts. After that, the team sends through the project's channels inside the founder's limits.
7. **One thing for the founder each day.** Size it for 15 to 30 minutes. If a day needs both a decision and a task, the decision goes first and the task moves to tomorrow.
8. **Close each day on the outcome.** Record what real thing exists now: an address, a sent message, a live post, a payment link, a reply. If the outcome did not happen, tomorrow's plan is the same outcome with a smaller first step, and the next day's work waits.

## What good looks like

A founder wrote: "I want to teach beginners to sew their own clothes." It is a service. The project can publish the page and send email. No repository, social account or payment tool is connected.

Day 1: the page goes live at its free address with "Join the first class list" as its one step. The founder texts the link to a cousin.
Day 2: the team writes "for people who own a sewing machine they have never used" and a three-line message. The founder sends it to five people from a local craft group.
Day 3: two people have joined the list. The team drafts a short note to them, the founder approves it, and it goes out from the project's address.
Day 4: with no social account connected, the team drafts a post and the founder puts it in the craft group herself.
Day 5: the team prepares one class at $25 with two other price options. The founder picks $25 and connects a payment tool.

By the end of the week the project has a live page, six names on its list and a link that can take a payment. No task was planned for a booking app, a company registration or ads.

## Output

```
Project kind: service, product, or software or content
Can do today: publish page y/n, send email y/n, publish social y/n, take payment y/n, keep code y/n
Today is day: <n> of the sequence
Today's outcome: <one line>
Team: <tasks to start, each able to finish with today's tools>
Founder's one thing: <15 to 30 minutes>
Done looks like: <what will exist outside the product>
Left for later: <task>: <one-line reason>
Stuck, said once: <what is missing and what would fix it>
```

## Check before you hand it over

- Every task in today's plan can finish with a tool marked yes.
- The founder has one thing today, and it is the most important one.
- Nothing from the "later" list in step 3 is in the plan.
- No code task is planned while "keep code" is no.
- Today's outcome is something outside the product. A simulated result is not counted.
- The plan fits the limit on agents working at once and the remaining budget.
- Nothing in the plan suggests one person is too few.

## What needs the founder

Publishing the page, sending anything from their own accounts, approving any message the team sends to people outside, deciding the price, and opening or connecting the account that takes payment. Prepare each so it takes one read and one action.

## If a tool is missing

Plan the day's outcome the manual way and say that you did. With no publishing tool, hand the founder the finished page copy and say it is not live. With no sending tool, the founder sends the draft themselves. With no payment tool, day 5 ends with a booking by reply ("answer this message to hold a place") and a line saying no payment can be taken yet. A prepared draft is never counted as a real outcome.
