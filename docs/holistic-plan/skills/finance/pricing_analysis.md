# Pricing

## When to use this

Use this to set a first price when there is no sales data, to design a founding-customer price, to review a price once there are sales, or to decide what an upgrade prompt says and when it appears. It works for a service, a physical product and software.

It ends in a recommendation. Setting or changing a live price is the founder's decision.

## Before you start

Read these first:

1. What is being sold, to whom, and what they get.
2. The cost records: materials, packaging, postage, tools, fees, and the time one sale takes.
3. What customers and followers have said about money, in conversation notes, replies and sales records.
4. Any price already shown in public. A price people have seen is a live price.

A price has three parts. Decide each one on purpose.

| Part | Question | Default for a first offer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is included | What does the buyer get? | One offer. Add a second only when buyers ask for more or for less. |
| The unit | Charged per item, per hour, per visit, per month, per person or per project? | The unit the buyer already thinks in. Someone who uses more of it should get more from it. |
| The amount | How much? | The steps below |

## Steps

1. **Find the floor.** The floor is what one sale costs the project. Below it every sale loses money, and it is never the price itself.

   | Selling | Cost of one sale |
   | --- | --- |
   | A service | Hours of work, travel and admin at the hourly figure the founder needs, plus materials |
   | A physical product | Materials, packaging, postage, the share that breaks or comes back, and making time |
   | Software or a subscription | Hosting and support time for each customer |

   Add the payment tool's fee to every row. Read the current rate from the project's payment records or from the tool's own pricing page. Label every figure you did not read from a record as Estimate.
2. **Find what the alternatives cost.** The buyer compares the price with what they do today. List three to five alternatives, with doing it themselves and doing nothing among them. For each, give the price or the cost in time, the link, and the date you read it. A competitor's price is one data point. Their costs and their customers are different, so their number is not a target.
3. **Work out what it is worth to the buyer.** Put it in their terms: hours saved, a worry gone, something they could not do before. Use their own words from the records. This is the ceiling.
4. **Pick a price to learn from.** It sits between the alternatives and the ceiling, with clear room above the floor. With no sales data the first price is a bet, and its purpose is a clear reaction from real buyers. Two rules of thumb:
   - A round number a little higher teaches more than a clever low one. A very low price attracts people who would never pay a workable price, and a price is harder to move up later than down.
   - If no price above the floor looks believable next to the alternatives, the offer has to change before the number does.
5. **Set three price points to test in conversations.** The middle one is the price from step 4. The low one is the lowest the project could live with. The high one is about half again above the middle. Each person hears one price, stated plainly with what it covers, and then silence. Record the reaction in their words.

   | Reaction | Read it as |
   | --- | --- |
   | Yes at once, or "is that all?" | Too low for this person |
   | A question about what is covered, then yes | About right |
   | A pause, or "I'd have to think about it" | Near their limit, or the worth is not clear yet |
   | No, with a reason | Keep the reason; it teaches more than the no |

   Ask what they paid the last time they dealt with this. What people did predicts better than what they say they would do. A pre-order or a booked date is a stronger yes than any sentence, and taking either is a separate step for the founder to decide.

   With ten or more conversations, four questions give a range: at what price would it seem too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider. The answers show how the price is seen. They do not show how many people would buy.
6. **Design a founding-customer price.** This is a lower price for the first customers in return for their feedback. It has four parts, each one written down and decided by the founder:
   - The regular price, shown beside it.
   - A real limit: a number of places or an end date that is kept to.
   - What the founding customer gives back: a frank conversation about how it went.
   - What happens afterwards: how long they keep the price.

   Never present a limit that is not real.
7. **Work out what the price has to do.** Margin on one sale is the price minus the cost of one sale. Sales needed in a month are the fixed monthly costs divided by that margin. Show the arithmetic and label the result Estimate.
8. **Review a live price on evidence.** Signs that a higher price is worth proposing: buyers say yes without a pause, people call it cheap, the offer now covers more than when the price was set, or demand is more than the founder's time allows. Signs to look at the offer first: people say it is not worth it, or buy once and never return. A proposal gives the order of change: new customers first, then existing customers with plenty of notice, the reason in one sentence, and what they keep.
9. **Write upgrade prompts that take no for an answer.** An upgrade prompt is the screen or message that invites someone on a free or smaller plan to pay, or to pay more.

   | An upgrade prompt may | It may never |
   | --- | --- |
   | Appear after the person has had their first result, at a real limit, or when they reach for a paid feature | Appear before they have got anything, or cut into a task they are in the middle of |
   | State what the paid option adds and what it costs | Hide the price or the way to close it |
   | Show "Not now" as clearly as the paid button | Use guilt, such as "No thanks, I don't want to grow" |
   | Return after a few days | Return in the same visit after "Not now" |
   | Give notice before a trial ends, with what happens next | Show a countdown or a "3 left" that is not real |
   | Lead to paying in a step or two | Charge without a final step the person takes on purpose |

## What good looks like

Lena mends and alters clothes at home. She has no sales yet and wants a price for hemming a pair of pants.

Floor: 25 minutes of work. At the $30 an hour Lena wrote that she needs, that is $12.50, plus thread at about $0.50 (Estimate). The payment tool's fee is not in the records yet: rate not read. Floor: $13 before that fee.
Alternatives: the dry cleaner on Main Street lists hemming at $18 with a one-week wait (its price list, read Nov 12, 2026). A tailor downtown lists $25 (its site, read the same day). Doing it at home costs an evening.
Worth to the buyer: pants that fit by the weekend without a trip downtown. Two followers wrote that the wait is what bothers them.
Price to learn from: $22 with a three-day turnaround.
Test points: $18, $22 and $32.
Founding price: $16 for the first ten customers, with $22 shown beside it, in return for ten minutes of frank feedback at pickup.
What it has to do: margin of $9 at $22 (Estimate). Lena's notes show $45 a month in fixed costs, so five hems a month cover them (Estimate).
Recommendation: $22, with the founding price for ten customers. If four of the first five people say yes to $32 without a pause, test higher.

## Output

Hand back:

```
Decision: what is being priced, for whom, and which kind (first price, founding price, review, upgrade prompt)
Floor: the cost of one sale line by line, each figure with its record or the label Estimate
Alternatives: name, price or cost in time, link, date read
Worth to the buyer: in their words, with the record
Recommended price and unit, and what it covers
Test points: low, middle and high, with the sentence the founder says
Founding-customer price: the four parts, or "not proposed" with the reason
What the price has to do: margin on one sale and sales needed in a month, labeled Estimate
What would change this recommendation
For a review: the signs seen, each with its record, and the proposed order of change
```

## Check before you hand it over

- Every number comes from a named record or is labeled Estimate, with the arithmetic shown.
- Each alternative has a link and a date.
- The price is above the floor, and the founder's time is counted in the floor.
- There is one offer and one unit, unless the records show buyers asking for more or for less.
- Reactions to a price are quoted in the person's words.
- Any limit on a founding price is real and kept to.
- Nothing in an upgrade prompt comes from the "may never" column.
- The hand-back recommends. It never reports a price as changed.

## What needs the founder

Setting a price, changing one, offering a discount or a founding price, taking a pre-order, and anything buyers are shown about money. Send the recommendation to the founder as one Needs you decision, with the options and your pick. Never change a price on a page or in a payment tool yourself.

## If a tool is missing

Without web research, list the alternatives the records mention, mark the rest "not checked", and say what to look up. Without payment records, every cost is an Estimate and the fee line reads "rate not read". In both cases the recommendation still stands on the floor and on what buyers said.
