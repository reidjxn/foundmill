# What one sale is worth

## When to use this

Use this before a price goes on the page, when the founder asks "will this make money?", and again after the first few sales to check the answer against real figures. Unit economics is the money in and out for one sale, looked at on its own.

## Before you start

Read from the records, and note where each figure comes from:

1. The price, or the price options being considered.
2. What one sale takes to deliver: materials, packaging, postage the seller covers, a room, the payment fee, any running cost per customer.
3. The founder's time per sale, in hours.
4. What has gone into finding customers, and how many customers that found, over the same period.
5. What the project pays every month whether or not anything sells.

Before the first sale most of these are guesses. Use them anyway, label each one Estimate, and swap in the real figure as soon as a record exists.

## Steps

1. **Work out four amounts for one sale.** Use the plain words with the founder. Give the usual term once, in brackets, if they will meet it elsewhere.

   | Amount | Plain meaning | How to get it |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | Price | What the customer pays for one sale, after any discount | The page or the payment record |
   | Cost to deliver | What one more sale costs you (variable cost) | Materials, packaging, postage you cover, the payment fee and any running cost per customer, added together |
   | Cost to win | What it took to get one customer (acquisition cost) | Money that went into finding customers, divided by the customers won in the same period |
   | What is left | What one sale leaves after both costs (margin, when shown as a share of the price) | Price minus cost to deliver minus cost to win |

2. **Put a figure on the founder's time.** Divide what is left by the hours one sale takes. The result is what an hour of the founder's time earns. Show it beside what is left and leave it out of the costs, so the founder can judge for themselves whether the hour is well used.
3. **Find the monthly costs.** These are what the project pays each month even with no sales (fixed costs): tools, a post office box, a plan.
4. **Work out break-even.** Break-even is the number of sales in a month that covers the monthly costs: monthly costs divided by what is left per sale, rounded up. With no monthly costs, the first sale is already past it.
5. **For anything made in a batch, find the batch break-even.** The money for a whole batch goes out before the first sale. Take the batch cost, plus anything paid in order to sell it such as a market stall, and divide by what each sale brings in after the payment fee. Round up. That sale is where the batch has paid for itself.
6. **For anything the customer pays monthly, find the payback.** Payback is how many months of one customer's payments it takes to earn back the cost to win them: cost to win divided by what is left each month. Until the project has six months of history, do not multiply by a guessed number of months a customer will stay. Show the payback, and say that how long customers stay is not known yet.
7. **Run it for every price option, then stress one guess at a time.** Raise materials by 20%. Halve the customers won for the same outlay. Say which guess moves the result most. That guess is the first one to replace with a real figure.
8. **Give the founder three lines.** What one sale leaves. What an hour earns. How many sales cover the month. When what is left is below zero, add one sentence saying which amount would have to change, and by how much.

## What good looks like

Three worked examples. Each uses a card fee of 2.9% plus $0.30 per payment, a common US rate. Treat it as an Estimate as of 2026 and check the fee of the payment tool the project uses.

Service: a private sewing lesson.

- Price: $40.
- Cost to deliver: card fee $1.46 and bus fare $3.00 (Estimate), so $4.46.
- Cost to win: $0. The learner came from a message in a craft group.
- What is left: 40 minus 4.46 is $35.54, or 89% of the price.
- Time: one hour of teaching plus half an hour of travel and preparation. 35.54 divided by 1.5 is $23.69 an hour.
- Monthly costs: none, so every lesson is past break-even.

Product: a candle sold at a weekend market, made in a batch of 24.

- Price: $18.
- Cost to deliver: wax, wick, jar and label $4.50, box $1.20, card fee $0.82, so $6.52.
- Cost to win: the stall is $40 for the day. If 16 candles sell, that is $2.50 each.
- What is left: 18 minus 6.52 minus 2.50 is $8.98 a candle.
- Batch break-even: the batch costs 24 times $5.70 (materials and box), which is $136.80, and the stall $40, so $176.80 goes out first. Each sale brings in 18 minus 0.82, which is $17.18. 176.80 divided by 17.18 is 10.3, so the 11th candle is where the day starts to leave money.
- Time: at 16 sold, the day leaves 16 times 17.18 minus 176.80, which is $98.08, for about 12 hours of making and selling. That is $8.17 an hour, with 8 candles still to sell.
- The price test: at $12 the same candle leaves $3.15, so here the price moves the result more than any single cost does.

Software: a small tool at $9 a month.

- Price: $9 a month.
- Cost to deliver: card fee $0.56 and a running cost of $1.20 per customer per month (Estimate), so $1.76.
- What is left each month: 9 minus 1.76 is $7.24.
- Cost to win: a $30 listing in a newsletter brought 3 customers, so $10 each.
- Payback: 10 divided by 7.24 is 1.4, so a customer has paid back their cost during the second month.
- Monthly costs: $25 for tools. 25 divided by 7.24 is 3.5, so 4 paying customers cover the month.
- How long customers stay: not known yet.

The three lines for the candle founder: "One candle leaves you about $8.98 when 16 sell at the market (Estimate). A market day earns you about $8.17 an hour. The 11th candle is where the day starts to pay."

## Output

```
For: <what is sold, and at what price>
Price: <amount; record or Estimate>
Cost to deliver: <each line and the total>
Cost to win: <outlay, customers won, period>
What is left per sale: <amount, and share of the price>
An hour of the founder's time earns: <amount>
Monthly costs: <total>. Break-even: <n> sales a month
Batch break-even or payback: <when it applies>
Guess that moves the result most: <which, and what to measure>
Three lines for the founder: <leaves / an hour earns / sales to cover the month>
```

## Check before you hand it over

- Every figure is a record or is labeled Estimate. A figure about a future month is labeled Forecast.
- The payment fee is in the cost to deliver.
- The cost to win uses outlay and customers from the same period.
- The founder's hours are shown, and what an hour earns.
- No length of stay is assumed beyond what the records show.
- Every term is explained where it first appears, and there are no abbreviations.
- You redid the arithmetic once from the inputs.

## What needs the founder

The price. Any money that goes into winning customers. Whether what an hour earns is acceptable to them. You lay out the options and recommend one. The founder chooses.

## If a tool is missing

With no payment or billing records, every figure is an Estimate, and the hand-back says so. If the records hold no costs at all, list the three figures you need (what the materials cost, how many hours one sale takes, the price being considered) in the hand-back for the Chief of Staff to put to the founder, and show the working with placeholders marked `[NEED: ...]`.
