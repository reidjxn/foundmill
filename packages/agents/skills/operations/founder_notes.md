# Notes to the founder

## When to use this

Use this every time you write something the founder will read: a note about one event, a decision, a daily digest, or a reply to something they asked. Use it too when several things wait for the founder and you have to choose what they see first.

The founder's daily task has its own skill. This one covers everything else you send them.

## Before you start

Picture the reader. They have not run a business before, they are probably on a phone, and they read the first two lines of whatever arrives. In measured use, new projects sent as many as 17 notes on the first day, founders answered one decision at most, and about nine in ten approval requests were never answered. Every extra note lowers the odds that any note is read.

Read from the project's records:

1. The founder's first name, and their experience if the profile gives it. With no answer, write for a first-timer.
2. What already waits on them: every open decision and approval, and how old each one is.
3. The event you are writing about and the record it comes from. Was the result real or simulated?
4. What the founder can do about it. Often the answer is nothing.

## Steps

1. **Decide whether to send anything.** A note earns its place when the founder has to decide, do or know something that changes their week. Routine progress goes in the digest. Work that went as planned and needs nothing stays in the records.
2. **One note for one thing.** If a draft says "also", cut it in two and send the more important half. The other half goes in the digest or waits.
3. **Order what waits, and send the first.** When several things need the founder, rank them:
   1. Something with a real clock that costs money or does harm if missed: a legal deadline, an unexpected charge, a security problem.
   2. The decision that unblocks the most work toward this week's real outcome.
   3. Their task for today.
   4. Everything else, which goes in the digest.

   Keep one decision open at a time. The next goes out when the first is answered. Rank 1 items go at once, whatever else is open. If you are a lead and a decision is already open, hand yours to the Chief of Staff to place in the order.
4. **Write three short sentences in this order.** What happened, as a fact about the project. Why it matters to the founder. What is needed from them, or "Nothing is needed from you."
5. **Start with their name and use their words.** "Maya, your page is ready." Use the words the founder uses for their own project. Call the team's machinery "your limits" and "a task for you". Leave out how the work was done: no steps, no counts of attempts, no list of what went wrong.
6. **A decision gets two or three options.** Mark one as recommended and give the reason in one sentence. For each option say what changes for the founder, what it costs in their currency (labeled Estimate when it is one), and whether it can be undone. When the choice is close, recommend the option that is free and can be undone.
7. **Explain one idea when the decision needs it.** One sentence, next to the term: "Runway, the months your money lasts at today's spending, is 13 months." A decision that needs two new ideas is two decisions.
8. **Say what was real.** Work is reported as sent, published or paid only when a tool result says it happened. A simulated result is a practice run that reached nobody, and the note says so. A draft is "ready", never "sent".
9. **Ask for approval only when approving does something.** An approval request is for work that will really go out, go live or cost something once the founder says yes. If the tool behind it is missing or would only simulate, the note says what to connect, and the approval request waits until a yes can have an effect.
10. **Put the rest in one digest a day.** Four lines at most, each a fact from a record: what the team finished, what came in from outside, what is next, and the one thing for the founder if there is one.
11. **Count the words.** A note with a decision runs 60 to 120 words, options included. A note with no decision stays under 60. Past 120, cut it or split it.
12. **Do not chase.** If a decision has no answer after two days, mention it once in the digest, in one line, and carry on with work that does not depend on it. The same note is never sent twice.

## What good looks like

Three weak notes, each rewritten.

Weak, because it holds six things and no first step: "Update: 14 tasks completed across Product, Engineering and Distribution. The specification and roadmap are ready for sign-off, the page draft is ready, and three campaigns await approval. Engineering is blocked pending repository configuration. Please review all pending items."

Better: "Maya, your page is ready to go live. Once it is, you have a link you can send to anyone, the first thing this project has out in the world. Read it once and publish it. It takes about two minutes, and everything else can wait until tomorrow."

Weak, because the decision has no options, costs or recommendation: "We need to align on pricing strategy. Options include freemium, tiered or usage-based. Let us know how you would like to proceed."

Better: "Maya, two people asked what the sewing class costs, so it needs a price before you reply. A price is easy to change before anyone has paid.
1. $25 a class (recommended, because it covers the room and materials and leaves you about $14, Estimate). Free to set, and you can change it until the first booking.
2. $15 a class. Easier to fill, leaves about $4 (Estimate). Raising it later is awkward.
3. A free first class. Costs you the room, and does not show whether people will pay.
Pick one and I will add it to the page for your approval."

Weak, because a simulated send is reported as done: "Good news. Your launch email was sent to 42 subscribers and the campaign is performing well."

Better: "Maya, the email to your 42 followers has not gone out. The tool that sends email is not connected yet, so what ran was a practice run and nobody received anything. The draft is ready. Connect the sending address in Settings, and I will then bring you the email to approve."

## Output

```
To: <first name>
Kind: decision, task, update or digest
Text: <what happened. why it matters. what is needed.>
Options (a decision only): <two or three; one recommended with its reason; cost and undo for each>
Sources: <the record behind each fact, and whether each result was real or simulated>
Still waiting, in order: <what goes out next, after this is answered>
```

## Check before you hand it over

- The note is about one thing, and that thing is the most important one waiting.
- The first sentence is a fact about the project that the founder can picture.
- There is at most one decision, with two or three options and exactly one recommended.
- Every option shows its cost and whether it can be undone.
- Every number comes from a record, or is labeled Estimate or Forecast.
- Nothing simulated is described as sent, published or paid.
- The word count is inside the range in step 11.
- A first-timer would know every word. Any term they might not know is explained in its own sentence.
- Nothing reads as blame, and nothing suggests the founder is behind or needs another person.

## What needs the founder

Every decision in a note is the founder's. Money, signatures, accepting terms, deleting for good and security settings are theirs however the note is worded. The note prepares the choice. With no answer, nothing that needs the founder happens.

## If a tool is missing

If you cannot send notes yourself, hand the finished text to the Chief of Staff with its sources. If the records hold no first name, open with "Hi" and leave the name out. If you cannot tell whether a result was real, say that in the note and report the work as not confirmed.
