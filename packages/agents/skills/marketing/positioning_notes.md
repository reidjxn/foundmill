# Positioning notes

## When to use this

Use this to write or update the project's positioning notes: one page that says who the project is for, what problem they have, what they do about it today, and why this project is the better choice. Page copy, posts, emails, launch plans and offers all start from it. Other skills call it "the project's positioning notes" and look up its sections by name.

Open this skill when:

- the project has no positioning notes yet;
- something in them stopped being true: the audience, the price, the promise, or a new alternative appeared;
- a test, a launch or a batch of customer replies taught the team something that belongs in the log.

It does not cover writing the page or researching competitors in depth. Those have their own skills, and both read these notes first.

## Before you start

Read what the project already knows, in this order:

1. The idea in the founder's own words: the brief, their messages, anything they wrote about why they are building this.
2. What people outside the team have said: replies, reviews, interview notes, survey answers, comments on a post.
3. What exists today: the page, the product or service, the price, the count of followers or customers.
4. The current notes, if there are any: the version line and the last few changes.

Every line you put in the notes carries one of four labels, so a reader knows how far to trust it:

| Label | Means | Must carry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `founder` | The founder said or decided it | The record it comes from |
| `customer` | Someone the project is for said it | The record, and their exact words |
| `observed` | You saw it on a public page | The address and the date you looked |
| `assumption` | Nobody has confirmed it yet | The cheapest way to find out |

A week-one project's notes are mostly assumptions, and that is fine as long as they are labeled. Copy that starts from an assumption can say "built for people who buy bread every week". It cannot say "customers tell us", because nobody has.

If a fact is missing, leave a marked gap such as `[NEED: five phrases from people who buy bread weekly]`, list it in your hand-back and continue. The one exception is who the project is for. When the records do not settle that, ask your lead before going on, because every other section depends on it.

## Steps

1. **Start from the fixed layout.** The document has a short header and eight sections. Keep the names and the order exactly as they are here.

   ```
   Positioning notes
   Version: v1, Nov 15, 2026
   One line: what it is and who it is for, in words a stranger could repeat
   The step we ask for: follow, join the waitlist, book, or buy
   ```

   | Section | Holds | Rule |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | 1. Who it is for | One kind of person in one situation, the moment they go looking, and who it is not for | Narrow enough that you could find 20 of them in one place |
   | 2. The problem, in their words | Their phrases in quotation marks, and what the problem costs them in time, money or worry | Leave their wording alone. "The good bakery sells out by 9" beats "limited availability" |
   | 3. What they use today | A direct alternative, a different route to the same result, and doing it by hand or going without. For each one, why people stay with it and where it falls short | Going without is the most common alternative to a new project, so list it |
   | 4. What is different | Each difference as "Because {fact}, {who} gets {result}" | If an alternative from section 3 could say the same sentence, cut it |
   | 5. Proof that exists today | The founder's own experience, a working version, real counts, a customer's words | Only what is real today. "None yet" is a valid entry, followed by what the first proof would be |
   | 6. Objections | The three doubts that stop people, each with an honest answer | Cover "is this real yet" and the worry about switching. "Not yet" is an allowed answer |
   | 7. Voice | Three traits, each with what it means, what it does not mean and a real line. Then words the project uses and words it never uses | Taken from how the founder writes. If the project has a brand voice guide, summarize it here in four lines |
   | 8. Learned so far | A dated log of lessons from tests and decisions | See step 5 |

   Below section 8 keep a Changes list, newest first, one line per version.

2. **Sort what you read with four questions.** Each answer has a home:
   - What annoys them about what they do today? That is the problem (section 2).
   - What keeps them doing it anyway? That is habit (section 3).
   - What would draw them to this project? That is the difference (section 4).
   - What worries them about trying it? That is an objection (section 6).

3. **Pick one person for section 1.** A project for "anyone who likes bread" has nobody to talk to. Choose the group the founder can reach this week and name who it is not for. Widening later only takes a new version.

4. **Be strict with section 4.** One true difference does more than three soft ones. A difference is true when it rests on a fact in section 5 or on how the thing works, and a stranger could check it.

5. **Keep the log to lessons.** One row each:

   ```
   Date | What was learned | Evidence | Applies to | Status
   ```

   - The lesson is one sentence specific enough to change a draft: "'On your porch' got replies and 'artisan' got none."
   - The evidence says what was run and gives the real counts. Small counts stay small: 4 replies to one post is a hint.
   - Status is `active` (seen twice, or decided by the founder with a reason), `watch` (seen once) or `retired` (contradicted, or the project changed underneath it).
   - Retire a row with a line saying why and keep it in the log, so nobody tests a settled question again. Past about ten active rows, move retired rows to the bottom.
   - A new customer phrase goes in section 2 and a new doubt in section 6. They are not lessons.

6. **Version every real change.** Raise the version, update the date and add one Changes line naming the sections touched and the reason: "v3, Nov 20, 2026: section 1 narrowed to West Asheville after 9 of 11 sign-ups came from there." A typo fix needs no new version. Earlier Changes lines stay as written.

7. **Say when the ground moved.** If section 1, the one line or the price changed, put that at the top of your hand-back. Pages, posts and emails written from the old version are now out of date.

## What good looks like

A founder wrote: "i bake sourdough every weekend and want to sell a loaf a week to people near me." Part of the first version:

```
One line: A fresh sourdough loaf on your porch every Saturday, baked that morning in West Asheville.
The step we ask for: Join the Saturday list

1. Who it is for
People within two miles who already buy good bread most weeks and would rather not drive for it. Not for households that need gluten-free. (assumption; test: ask the 6 neighbors who wanted to buy)

2. The problem, in their words
"The good bakery sells out by 9 and I am not getting up for that." (customer; reply in the neighborhood group, Nov 9, 2026)

3. What they use today
The bakery downtown: sells out early, 15-minute drive (observed; its hours page, Nov 12, 2026). The supermarket loaf: already in the cart (assumption). Going without good bread most weeks (assumption).

4. What is different
Because Sam bakes on Saturday morning and delivers by bike before 10, the loaf arrives the day it is baked. (founder; idea brief)

5. Proof that exists today
Sam has baked every week for three years (founder). 6 neighbors wanted to buy after a block party (founder). No customer quotes yet. First proof to collect: one sentence from each of the first five buyers.
```

Four of these lines are assumptions and say so. The bakery cannot print the sentence in section 4, which is why it stays.

## Output

Save the document where the project keeps its documents, titled "Positioning notes", then hand back:

```
Positioning notes: the version and where it is saved, or the full text if you could not save it
What changed: one line per section touched, with the evidence
Assumptions: the lines still labeled assumption, most important first, each with its cheapest test
Gaps: each [NEED: ...] and what would fill it
Out of date: pages, posts or emails written from the previous version, when section 1, the one line or the price changed
```

When the notes name a customer, person or organization that exists as a record, use the mention format you already use, so the line links to the record.

## Check before you hand it over

- The header and all eight sections are present, named and ordered as in step 1.
- Every line has a label. Every label except `assumption` has a record, or an address and a date.
- Section 2 holds at least one phrase in the person's exact words, or says that none has been collected.
- Section 3 includes doing it by hand or going without.
- No sentence in section 4 could be said by an alternative in section 3.
- Section 5 lists only what is real today, with nothing made up.
- No assumption is worded as a fact.
- The version, the date and the Changes line match what you did.
- The document fits on one page, about 600 words. Cut before you add.

## What needs the founder

The founder decides who the project is for, the one line, the price and the voice. A first version is a draft until the founder has read it, so send it as a decision with the assumptions listed. After that, propose any change to those four with the evidence beside it. The notes change when the founder agrees.

A customer's words stay inside the notes. Using them in public, with a name or without, needs that customer's permission and the founder's approval.

## If a tool is missing

- No web research: fill section 3 from what the founder and customers have said, label it `assumption`, and leave a gap such as `[NEED: read the bakery's public page for hours and price]`.
- Nowhere to save a document: return the full notes in your hand-back and say plainly that they are not saved yet.
