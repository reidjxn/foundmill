# Opening lines and subject lines

## When to use this

The opening is what people see before they decide to read: the first two lines of a post, the subject line of an email, the title of an article. Use this to write six openings for one topic, so the founder or the skill writing the rest can choose.

It does not write the body. And an opening never promises something the body does not deliver.

## Before you start

You need three things from the records:

1. **The topic,** and the piece the opening is for, if it exists yet.
2. **The facts available.** Figures, names, dates and what happened, each with its record. An opening is only as strong as the most specific true fact in it.
3. **What the founder personally did or saw.** An opening in the first person ("I", "How I") needs a real experience the founder supplied. Without one, work from the evidence and say the personal angle was not available.

Note where the opening will appear, because that sets its length:

| Where | Line 1 | Line 2 | Length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social post | The opening line | A line that turns it | 40 characters each |
| Email | Subject line | Preview text, the line a mail app shows after the subject | Subject near 40 characters, so a phone shows it whole |
| Article | Title | First sentence | Title under 60 characters |
| Short video | First spoken line | Words on screen | Both inside the first 3 seconds |

Phone and network cut-offs are as of 2026. Check the current value.

## Steps

1. **Build every opening as a pair.** Line 1 states something unexpected, specific or blunt. It is a statement, never a question. Line 2 contradicts it, reframes it, or cuts it down to size. The gap between the two is why someone reads the third line.
2. **Produce six, one for each angle.** Different angles suit different readers, and six gives a real choice.

   | Angle | Line 1 | It needs |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | Number | Leads with a figure | A figure from the records |
   | Contrarian | States a common belief, so line 2 can flip it | A view the founder holds and can back |
   | Before and after | Shows a change, with a digit | A real before and a real after |
   | A known name | Borrows a place, tool or brand the reader knows | A true connection to it |
   | Admission | Owns a mistake or a loss | Something that happened to the founder |
   | What changes next | Says what is about to be different | A dated fact, or a view marked as the founder's opinion |

   If an angle lacks what it needs, put "not available" and name the missing fact. Five honest openings are better than six with one made up.
3. **Use digits.** "3" and never "three". A digit stops the eye, and it has to be a real one.
4. **Keep each line inside its length.** Count the characters. A line that gets cut loses its point.
5. **Cut every word that carries nothing.** "Really", "just", "very", and any wind-up before the fact.
6. **Hold the tension honestly.** Curiosity may come from holding back the explanation, never from bending the truth. Keep the doubt the source had: if the record says "about 30", so does the opening. Never add a result, a deadline or a certainty the records do not hold.
7. **Run the promise test.** For each opening, put down in one line what the body must deliver for the reader to feel fairly treated. If the piece cannot deliver it, drop the opening.
8. **For an email, test the subject line twice more.** It should read like a message from someone the reader knows, and it should hold up if it is the only line they read. No fake "Re:" or "Fwd:", no capitals for emphasis, and "last chance" only when it truly is. The preview text continues the subject and never repeats it.
9. **Rank them.** Mark the one you would use and say why in a line. The choice belongs to the founder or to the skill that asked.

## What good looks like

Topic: this week's issue of Dev's newsletter on cheap food in Chicago. From his notes: he ate at 14 taco stands, every taco $3 or less, and a cart on 18th Street in Pilsen beat the $19 plate at a restaurant he had recommended for 2 years.

```
OPENINGS for "14 taco stands", for a social post

1. Number
14 taco stands. $3 or less each.
One beat a $19 restaurant plate.

2. Contrarian
Good tacos need a real kitchen.
The best one I ate came off a cart.

3. Before and after
For 2 years I sent friends to 1 place.
After 14 stands, I send them to a cart.

4. A known name
Pilsen has a taco cart on 18th.
It beat the place I recommended.

5. Admission
I recommended the wrong tacos.
For 2 years.

6. What changes next
Not available. [NEED: a dated fact about what is changing]

Pick: 1. Both figures are his, and line 2 makes line 1 matter.
Promise: the issue names the cart and the restaurant plate it beat.
```

Every line is 40 characters or fewer, and the sixth angle is left empty because the notes hold nothing about what comes next.

## Output

Hand back:

```
OPENINGS for <topic>, for <where it will appear>

1. Number
<line 1>
<line 2>

2. Contrarian
...through 6, each under its angle, or "Not available" with the missing fact

Pick: <which, and one line on why>
Promise: <for the pick, what the body must deliver>
Sources: the record behind each fact used
```

## Check before you hand it over

- All six angles are covered, or the missing ones name the fact they lack.
- Line 1 of each is a statement, with no question mark.
- Every line is inside its length. Count again.
- Every figure, name and event is in the records.
- First person appears only for the founder's own experience.
- No opening promises more than the piece delivers.
- No dashes, no filler words, and digits in place of spelled-out numbers.

## What needs the founder

The founder, or the skill that asked, picks the opening. An opening that names another person or business, or states an opinion as the founder's, needs the founder's approval before it is public. The post or email it belongs to goes through its own approval.

## If a tool is missing

This skill needs no tool. If you cannot count characters exactly, count by hand and say the count was by hand.

If the piece the opening is for does not exist yet, hand back the openings with the promise each one makes, so whoever writes the piece knows what it has to deliver.
