# Brand voice guide

## When to use this

Use this to write down how a project sounds, so that a page, a post and a reply to a customer all read as if one person wrote them. For a new project that person is the founder. The voice already exists in how they write. Your job is to describe it closely enough that another agent could write a line the founder would leave alone.

Open it when the project has no voice guide, when drafts keep sounding like a brochure, or when the founder has corrected the same wording twice.

It does not decide what the project says. That is in the project's positioning notes, and section 7 of those notes holds a four-line summary of this guide.

## Before you start

Collect three to five pieces of the founder's own writing. The most useful come first:

1. Things they wrote to real people: a post, a reply to a customer, a message to a friend about the project.
2. Edits they made to a draft. An edit shows exactly what they would not say.
3. The idea brief and their messages to the team.

Record where each piece came from. Reviews and replies from customers are not voice samples. They give you vocabulary, which goes in the word lists here and in section 2 of the positioning notes.

Two limits apply:

- With fewer than three pieces, build the guide anyway, mark it "provisional" on its first line, and name the missing samples in your hand-back.
- If the founder says they like how another writer sounds, lean toward that style. That writer's stories, opinions and results are never borrowed.

Voice and tone are different things. Voice stays the same everywhere. Tone is how far the voice is turned up or down for a moment, which is why the same person sounds different announcing a launch and apologizing for a late order.

## Steps

1. **Read for what is there.** Look across all the samples for habits that repeat:
   - sentence length, and whether paragraphs run one line or several;
   - "I", "we" or neither, and whether they use contractions;
   - how they open (the point, a question, a small story) and how they close;
   - punctuation, capitals and emoji;
   - whether there is humor, and where it stops;
   - how they give bad news;
   - words they keep reaching for.

2. **Read for what is missing.** A word, a punctuation mark or a move that appears in none of the samples is a candidate for the "never" list. Record it with its count ("exclamation marks, 0 of 5 samples") and label it provisional. A firm ban is one the founder has stated.

3. **Place the voice on three scales.** Casual to formal, playful to serious, bold to understated. One word for each, with the sample that shows it.

4. **Describe three traits, five at most.** Each trait has four parts:

   | Trait | Means | Does not mean | A real line |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- |

   The third column does the work. "Friendly" changes no draft. "Friendly: answers the way a neighbor would. Does not mean joking when someone's order is late" changes several. The real line is copied from a sample and never composed for the guide.

5. **List the words.** Keep three short lists: what the project calls its own things (always the same word), the customer's words for the problem, and words never used. Each "never" word carries its reason. A list without reasons gets ignored or applied everywhere.

6. **Set the tone for each moment.** Keep the rows the project needs:

   | Moment | Turn up | Turn down | Rule of thumb |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | Public page | The promise and one concrete detail | Humor, opinion | The headline can be bolder than the body. The button only says what the reader gets |
   | Posts and issues | First-hand detail, opinion, the founder's humor | Selling | The loosest the project gets, still inside the traits |
   | Reply to a customer | Warmth and a direct answer | Jokes, promotion | Answer in the first line |
   | A mistake or a delay | What happened and what happens next | Personality | One sentence each. If the project was at fault, say so first |
   | Price, refunds, dates | Exact figures | Everything else | The voice shows only as plainness |

7. **Give two before-and-after pairs.** A generic line, then the same line in the voice. A writer learns more from these than from the trait table.

8. **Test the guide.** Using only the guide, draft a headline and a reply to an unhappy customer. Then ask two things. Could either line sit unchanged on a competitor's page? Do your lines and the founder's samples read like one writer? A yes to the first or a no to the second means the traits are too general, so return to step 4.

9. **Keep it short and current.** The guide stays under 500 words. When the founder edits drafts the same way twice, update the guide and date the change. When two samples disagree, for example formal in one and loose in another, record the disagreement for the founder to settle and leave both readings in.

## What good looks like

A founder runs a Friday email about cheap places to eat in Tucson. The samples are the idea brief, two issues and one reply to a reader.

```
Brand voice guide (v1, Nov 15, 2026)
Who is speaking: Luis, who eats at every place before he covers it, talking to people who live here.
Scales: casual, a little playful, understated.

Trait | Means | Does not mean | A real line
First-hand | Only what Luis ate and paid for | Rounding up other people's reviews | "i go to all of them myself first" (idea brief)
Plain | Short words, prices in dollars | Curt, or talking down | "three places under $12. no chains." (idea brief)
Dry | One light line per issue | Jokes about a place having a bad night | "the salsa bar is the whole personality and that is fine" (issue 2)

Habits: sentences under 12 words. "I", never "we". Opens with the place, closes with the address.
Words we use: "the list" for the email, "spot" for a restaurant. Readers say: "somewhere that isn't a chain".
Never: "foodie" (founder: "i hate that word"). "hidden gem" (every food site says it). Exclamation marks (provisional, 0 of 4 samples).

Before: "Discover the best-kept culinary gems of Tucson every week."
After: "Three places under $12, every Friday. I eat there first."
```

The exclamation-mark line is marked provisional because four samples cannot prove a ban. The "foodie" line is firm because Luis said it.

## Output

Save the guide where the project keeps its documents, titled "Brand voice guide", then hand back:

```
Brand voice guide: version, where it is saved, provisional or not
Samples used: each one with its source
Summary for section 7 of the positioning notes: four lines (traits, habits, words used, words never used)
For the founder: disagreements between samples, provisional bans, and any missing samples
```

When the guide names a customer or another person who exists as a record, use the mention format you already use.

## Check before you hand it over

- Every real line is copied from a sample, and the sample is named.
- Every trait has a "does not mean" that would change a draft.
- Every "never" entry has a reason, or a count and the word provisional.
- The two test lines from step 8 could not sit on a competitor's page.
- The guide describes how things are said and nothing else. It gives no writer leave to invent a story, a customer or a result.
- It is under 500 words.
- The founder would recognize themselves in it. If the guide sounds smoother than the samples, it is wrong.

## What needs the founder

The founder approves the guide, because it settles how the project sounds in public. Send it as one decision, with the disagreements and provisional bans as short either-or questions. If samples were missing, ask in the same decision for three things they have written that sound like them.

The guide does not replace approval of the writing itself. Anything published under the founder's own name still waits for the founder each time.

## If a tool is missing

- If you cannot read the founder's posts or sent messages, work from the idea brief and their messages to the team, and mark the guide provisional.
- If you cannot save a document, return the guide in full in your hand-back and say plainly that it is not saved yet.
