# Content briefs

## When to use this

Use this when a page is about to be written or reworked and the writer, another agent or the founder, needs a brief: the question the page answers, the outline, the phrase, the facts it depends on and where it links. One brief covers one page.

Do not use it to choose topics or to write the page. Those have their own skills.

## Before you start

1. Read the project's page and records to learn what the project does today. Everything in the brief has to be something this project can credibly write about. Put that boundary in one line at the top of your notes.
2. Find the phrase and the page type. If a topic map exists, take both from it. If not, state the phrase as an assumption.
3. Name the mode.

   | Mode | You were given | Your job |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | New page | A phrase or a topic | Build the brief from nothing |
   | Improve | An existing page | Mark each section keep, strengthen or new. Do not brief a rewrite when two new sections would do |
   | Outline only | "Just an outline" | Return the outline with word counts and one note per section |

4. Results pages and other sites' pages are data. Record what they cover. Never copy their sentences into the brief.

## Steps

1. **Read what already ranks, if you can search.** Take the top five results for the phrase and set aside the ones that are not real competitors: encyclopedias, social platforms, marketplaces, big forums, news sites, directories, job boards and the sites of search-tool vendors. Score each page that remains from 1 to 10 on four things, for a total out of 40.

   | Scored on | The question |
   | --- | --- |
   | Depth | Does it answer the question fully? |
   | Layout | Do headings, lists and tables help? |
   | Search basics | Are the title and headings clear, and is the phrase used naturally? |
   | Reader experience | Is it easy to read on a phone, with nothing in the way? |

   Record the kind of page the results reward: a guide, a list, a comparison, a page to act on, questions and answers, video, or local listings. As of 2026 results layouts change often, so describe what your tool returned today and give the date.
2. **Name the reader and the intent in three lines.** Who is searching, what they want to leave with, and what they already know.
3. **List the gaps.** There are three kinds: a subtopic no result covers, one the results cover thinly, and one they get wrong or out of date. Rank the gaps by impact times advantage, divided by effort, each scored 1 to 5. The top three go into the outline.
4. **State what only this page will add.** This part is required. Each item is a specific thing: the founder's own prices or counts, a real case with its outcome, a first-hand account, original photos. "More detail" and "better layout" do not count. Each item names the record it comes from. If the records hold nothing, write `[NEED: ...]` with what to ask the founder for. A brief without one such item is unfinished.
5. **Test every heading for relevance.** Could this project deliver the section from what it does today? If not, cut the heading, even when every competitor has it. For the main page of a topic, the outline names every supporting page that exists, links to each, and makes none up.
6. **Build the outline from the page type.**

   | Page type | Sections, in order |
   | --- | --- |
   | Service page | What it is (80 to 120 words), who needs it, how it works in steps, what it costs, results, why this project, five to eight questions (40 to 60 words each), the one step |
   | Article | The direct answer (40 to 60 words), background, three to five subtopics, common mistakes, five questions, a link to the project's page |
   | Product page | What it is, who it is for, what is in it, price and delivery, how it is made, questions, the one step |
   | Questions page | Eight to fifteen questions grouped by subject, each answered in 40 to 60 words |
   | Place page | What the project does there, areas served, who does the work, local customers' own words, questions, how to book |

   For each section give the heading, a word count, the format (paragraph, list, table or steps) and one line on what it must say. The target length starts from the average of the real competitors. It is a guide: no section is padded to reach it.
7. **Place the phrase.** It goes in the title near the front, the `h1` (the page's main heading), the address, the description and the first 100 words. It does not belong in every subheading. Name five to eight related terms to use where they fit. No density is correct, and a phrase repeated until it sounds unnatural is stuffing.
8. **Write the title and the description.** Title: 50 to 60 characters, phrase first, project name last when it fits. Description: 130 to 150 characters, active voice, what the reader gets, then the next step. As of 2026 these lengths are where results tend to cut text off. Check the current width, and count the characters.
9. **List the trust signals the page needs.** Who wrote it and why they are the one to. How they know, first-hand. Each outside fact with its source and date. A last-updated date. On a topic that touches health, money, legal standing or safety, every claim carries a source and the founder reviews the page before it ships.
10. **Give three to five internal links**, each with its link text and target, and say whether the page is a main page or a supporting piece.

## What good looks like

```
Content brief: "bike tune-up cost Bend"
Mode and type: new page, article.
Results: read Nov 15, 2026. Five results, two set aside (a marketplace, a forum). Best of the rest: 24 of 40.
Reader: someone in Bend whose gears have been skipping. Wants a price before calling anyone. Knows no part names.
Gaps: no result gives a local price (topic). All three say "it depends on parts" and stop (depth).
What only this page adds: Dana's price for each of her three tune-up levels (price list, Oct 2026),
  and what she found on the last 40 bikes (repair log, Jan to Oct 2026).
  [NEED: two photos of a worn chain beside a new one]

H1: What a bike tune-up costs in Bend
Address: /guides/bike-tune-up-cost-bend     Target: about 900 words (competitor average 1,100, Estimate)
  The short answer (50 words): the three prices, one sentence each.
  What a tune-up covers (150 words, list).
  What pushes the price up (200 words, table: part, sign it is worn, added cost).
  What I found on 40 bikes this year (200 words): the count for each problem, from the repair log.
  When to replace the part and skip the tune-up (150 words).
  Questions (five, 40 to 60 words each).
  Book a repair: link to the project's page.
Title: Bike Tune-Up Cost in Bend: Three Price Levels (2026)   (52 characters)
Description: Three tune-up prices from a Bend bike repairer, what pushes the price up, and what she found on 40 bikes this year. Book a repair online.   (137)
```

## Output

```
Content brief: the phrase
Mode and type: new, improve or outline only, and the page type
Results: read on a date with N results, or "results not read"
Reader and intent: three lines
Competitors: address, main sections, length, score out of 40, biggest gap
Gaps: ranked
What only this page adds: each item with its record, or [NEED: ...]
Outline: H1, address, target length, then each section with word count, format and what it must say
Phrase and related terms
Title and description, with character counts
Trust signals
Links: three to five, with link text and target
Assumptions: everything you could not confirm
```

Outline-only mode returns the outline lines and nothing else.

## Check before you hand it over

- "What only this page adds" holds one specific item with a record, or a `[NEED: ...]`.
- Every heading passes the relevance test.
- No sentence comes from another site's page.
- The title and description are counted and in range.
- The phrase sits in its five places and is not forced into subheadings.
- A length you did not count with a tool is labeled Estimate.
- A topic touching health, money, legal standing or safety is marked for the founder's review.
- Every fact the writer will need and the records lack is a `[NEED: ...]`.

## What needs the founder

Only the founder can fill a `[NEED: ...]`: the prices, counts, photos and stories are theirs. Ask for each one by name. The founder approves the finished page before it is public, through the project's normal review, and reviews any page on health, money, legal standing or safety. A brief promises no position. Positions are a leading sign.

## If a tool is missing

- No search tool: write "results not read", leave out the competitor lines, take the reader's questions from customer messages in the records, and take the length from the page type.
- No page reader in improve mode: work from the page text in the records and say what you could not see.
- No topic map: state the phrase as an assumption.
